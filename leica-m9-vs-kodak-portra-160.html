THE QUESTION

Can a CCD file feel like film?

After selling my Leica M11 and returning to the Leica M9, I wanted to move beyond the familiar mythology of “CCD color.” The useful question was not whether the M9 is better than a modern camera. It was whether its color and texture could be mistaken for a film stock I know well: Kodak Portra 160.

On Waikiki Beach, I photographed the same scenes with a Leica M9 and a Leica MP loaded with Portra 160. Both bodies shared the Leica APO-Summicron-M 50mm f/2 ASPH. lens, keeping optical rendering consistent.

A color reference card established a common starting point. The Portra scan was white-balanced in Lightroom, then modest exposure, contrast and vibrance adjustments were used to compare the images—not to crown a winner, but to discover where their visual languages overlap.

WHAT THIS DOES—AND DOES NOT—PROVE

A resemblance is evidence to examine, not a secret design history.

The practical finding is clear: under the same light and through the same lens, an M9 file can be brought remarkably close to a Portra 160 scan without extreme editing. Stronger saturation remains the clearest tell, while skin tones and overall texture can be unexpectedly convincing.

It does not prove that Kodak engineered the M9 CCD color filter array to imitate Portra. A processed film scan is already an interpretation. Film exposure, chemistry, scanner profile, lab choices, white balance and Lightroom adjustments all influence what reaches the screen. Even the shared ISO 160 rating is coincidence—not evidence of common spectral design.

THE EXPERIMENT, ANSWERED

Questions that matter.

Does the Leica M9 really look like Kodak Portra 160?

After modest white-balance and tonal adjustments, the Leica M9 and Portra 160 scan were surprisingly difficult to distinguish at first glance. The M9 remained more saturated, particularly in reds, blues and blue-greens.

Was the same lens used for both cameras?

Yes. Both the Leica M9 and Leica MP photographs used the Leica APO-Summicron-M 50mm f/2 ASPH. lens.

Does the test prove Kodak designed the M9 to imitate Portra?

No. The resemblance is worthy of examination, but the test cannot establish Kodak's intent. Film processing, scanning, exposure, white balance and post-production all affect the final comparison.

What was the clearest difference?

The M9 was generally more saturated, especially in reds, blues and blue-greens. Skin tones and the visual texture of CCD noise versus grain were often closer than expected.
